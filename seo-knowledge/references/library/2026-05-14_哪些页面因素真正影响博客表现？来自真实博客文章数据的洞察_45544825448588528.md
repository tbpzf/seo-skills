# What On-Page Factors Actually Impact Blog Performance? Insights from Real Blog Post Data

- Content Marketing, SEO

- [Chandler Sullivan](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/author/chandler/)

- April 1, 2026

![Mock blog post and GSC data showing different data clusters of blogs](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Factors-that-impact-blog-performance-Insights-from-Real-Blog-Post-Data.jpg)

Share Article

If you’ve spent any time in [content marketing](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/services/seo-content-marketing/), you’ve probably heard the same advice: “Write 2,000 words”, “Add FAQs”, or “Use more keywords” when it comes to writing high-engaging blog content.

The reality is, blog performance is far more nuanced. What works for one topic, audience, or search query may not work for another: some people enjoy long, in-depth articles, while others prefer to skim a short news post. And as search evolves—especially with the rise of [AI-driven results](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/adapting-your-content-strategy-for-ai-llm-visibility/)—it’s becoming increasingly clear that there is no single formula for success. But when you analyze real performance data across multiple blogs, patterns begin to emerge.

At SocialSurge, we wanted to move beyond assumptions and look at what actually drives performance. So, we analyzed 50+ blog posts across multiple industries to identify what actually moves the needle.

The question we set out to answer: ***which on-page blog characteristics are most consistently associated with stronger organic visibility?***

## A Quick Note Before We Dive Into Our Findings

**This wasn’t a controlled experiment—it was an observational analysis based on real businesses, real content, and real search environments.**

In a perfect world, we’d isolate one variable at a time, like only testing images or only testing word count. However, that is not how real marketing works. Many on-page (and [technical SEO](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/services/milwaukee-seo-company/technical-seo/)) factors can impact a post’s performance, so it is hard to pinpoint the **absolute** influence.

That means there are variables we couldn’t isolate:

1. **Topic Demand:** Some topics simply have more search volume than others. This is a good reminder that no amount of formatting can overcome low search demand. Topic demand sets the ceiling. Structure determines how close you get to it.
2. **Content Age & Indexing:** Not all blogs had equal time to perform. Some were newly published, while others had months of authority or were previously updated (and the link between [rewrites and blog performance is something we’ve analyzed before](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/rewriting-content-smarter-seo-content-strategy/)). This is especially important when considering low-impression outliers, which, in many cases, were simply too new to evaluate fairly.
3. **External Factors:** The timeframe analyzed (Sept 2025–Jan 2026) included [algorithm updates and increased AI-driven SERP features](https://searchengineland.com/why-google-search-console-impressions-dropped-interpret-data-463677), which *likely* influenced visibility.

Even with all these variables, patterns still emerged. And when the same behaviors show up across industries, they’re worth paying attention to.

## What On-Page Factors *Actually* Impact Blog Performance?

We analyzed Google Search Console (GSC) data from between **September 2025 and January 2026**, evaluating:

- Organic impressions and clicks
- Content structure and formatting
- Image usage (or image count)
- Word count
- Use of FAQs, Q&A, and heading hierarchy
- Presence of research, stats, citations, bios, reviews, and external links ([EEAT signals](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/google-e-e-a-t-how-to-demonstrate-first-hand-experience/474446/))

Here is what stood out:

### 1. The Biggest Finding: Structure Beats Length Every Time

**There was no clear correlation between word count and performance.**

Across all blog posts:

- High-performing blogs ranged from ~750 to 2,000 words
- Low-performing blogs existed within that same range

The difference was in the structure of blog posts and the on-page characteristics.

We found that higher-performing posts consistently had:

- Clear H2 and H3 hierarchy
- Bullet points and lists
- Short, digestible sections
- Logical progression of ideas
- Answers to common questions for that topic
- Multiple supporting images (not including the featured image)

Lower-performing content tended to rely on dense blocks of text with minimal formatting, and a lack of supporting images—making it harder to read, harder to scan, and ultimately easier to ignore.

![](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Blog-formatting-differences.jpg)

This aligns with a principle from usability expert Steve Krug in his book, *[Don’t Make Me Think, Revisited: A Common Sense Approach to Web Usability (3rd Edition)](https://www.amazon.com/Dont-Make-Think-Revisited-Usability/dp/0321965515)*, where he talks about how people don’t read pages—they scan them.

That behavior doesn’t just apply to websites; it also impacts how people engage with blog content. And increasingly, it affects how search engines and AI interpret content.

### 2. The Most Consistent Signal: Image Usage—With a Catch

**One of the most consistent patterns across high-performing content was the use of meaningful, well-placed visuals.**

Top-performing posts typically had:

- 3–5 images (excluding featured image)
- Visuals that added context (charts, infographics, examples)
- Images placed throughout the posts, not just in one spot

Lower-performing posts typically had:

- 0–1 image total (excluding featured image)
- Generic or non-contextual visuals

I want to be clear: It’s not just about adding images, it’s about adding *useful* images. Decorative images don’t improve understanding, reinforce concepts, or support action. And based on this data, they don’t improve performance either.

![](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Different-blog-data-point-clusters.jpg)

### 3. FAQs Aren’t Always the Answer, But Answering Questions Is

**This might be one of the more nuanced findings: FAQs alone were not a consistent ranking factor.**

Some of the highest-performing posts:

- Had no formal FAQ section
- Still performed exceptionally well

So what did they do instead? They answered questions directly, within the structure of the blog.

This looked like:

- Headers framed as questions (“What happens when…”, “How to…”)
- Immediate, clear answers following those headers

This approach aligns more naturally with search intent and improves readability without relying on a formal FAQ block. This doesn’t mean you should start deleting FAQs within a post; this is just another way to include search queries without a dedicated FAQ section.

### 4. EEAT Still Matters, And It Shows

**[EEAT signals](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/beginners-guide-to-seo/link-building/) are still an important on-page factor.**

High-performing posts often included:

- External links and citations
- Statistics and research
- Real-world examples
- Industry-specific insights
- First-hand quotes

![diagram explaining EEAT experience expertise authoritativeness trustworthiness](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/EEAT-experience-expertise-authoritativeness-trustworthiness-diagram.jpg)

Lower-performing posts often:

- Relied only on internal knowledge (or data)
- Lacked supporting data altogether
- Felt more generic or surface-level

Additionally, as search engines (and AI) continue to prioritize trust and accuracy, unsupported content is less likely to stand out.

### 5. The Reality Check: Topic Demand Still Wins

**At the end of the day, no matter how well a blog post is structured, it cannot outperform its demand.**

In this dataset:

- A high-demand topic generated 375K+ impressions
- Some niche topics struggled to reach even 1K

Even well-written, well-structured blogs underperformed when targeting low-demand queries.

The takeaway here is that great structure improves performance, but it can’t create demand where none exists. You can’t just “create” a problem or create a “solution” to a problem that doesn’t exist, so don’t try to do that with your content. Furthermore, the [“quality” of a blog post must align with the intent.](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/does-ai-actually-reward-quality-content/570627/?utm_source=www.aimarketers.co&utm_medium=newsletter&utm_campaign=your-ai-in-marketing-news-from-the-latest-week-april-26-2026&_bhlid=5c15e6803d19d479341c560933c8bf6070b882b4)

Of course, there are exceptions to this when branding pieces or proprietary terms need to be discussed, but I wouldn’t expect those posts to be the highest-performing. Overall, doing [research on what your target audience actually wants to read](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/b2b-user-journey-seo-strategy-guide/) about is important from the start.

## Recap: What High-Performing Blog Posts *Actually* Have in Common

Across all industries and blog post types, the strongest performers shared:

- Clear, scannable formatting (H2s, H3s, bullets)
- 3–5 meaningful, context-driven images
- Answer-focused headings aligned with search intent
- Credibility signals (research, stats, external links)
- Alignment with real search demand

No single factor drove success, but rather it was a combination of all these things.

## What This Means for Your Content Strategy

If you’re creating blog content today, here’s where to focus:

Stop over-optimizing for:

- Word count targets
- Keyword density
- “Perfect” blog formulas

![target audience infographic](https://socialsurgemarketing.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/Understand-your-target-audience-1024x538.jpg)

Start prioritizing:

- Structure and readability
- Content that’s easy to interpret (for both users and AI)
- Visual support that adds value
- Clear, answer-first writing
- Credibility and depth where it matters
- Topic selection based on real search demand
- Alignment between content type and query intent

Not every blog will be a top performer, but that shouldn’t be your goal. The goal is to consistently create content that is relevant, useful, and aligned with how your audience actually searches.
