# How To Face Your Content Debt and Get Buy-In for Fixing It

"Same as it ever was” doesn’t work for content libraries anymore — publishing without maintaining only builds content debt. Here’s how to stop the problem from compounding.

When I [recently told you](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/strategy-planning/web-traffic-burn) to let the old click economy burn, several of you asked, “Which content do we burn first?”

That’s a great question because it shows a fundamental awareness that building a content-led machine is one thing. Maintaining the building that houses it is an entirely separate challenge.

To tackle it, you’ll have to face your content debt.

I’ll share a process to help you do that. But first, I want to show you something hidden on your website right now.

## A lifetime of content

Three or more clicks deep in your website’s resource center (or linked from some ancient post) lives a pricing PDF from 2021.

It uses outdated product names and pricing tiers from two packaging changes ago. No one on your current team knows it exists, but it still ranks on Google. And a prospect’s AI search agent downloaded it last Tuesday.

Keep spelunking, and you’ll meet that document’s neighbors: 15 e-books that each describe your core value proposition a little differently, depending on which agency wrote them in which fiscal year.

Related:[Old Content, New Risk: What’s AI Learning About Your Brand?](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/content-operations/old-content-new-risk-ai)

You might bump into the 2019 webinar recording that hyped up 5G or predicted we’d all be undergoing mobile robot surgery within two years.

Then there’s your site search, which faithfully indexes 400 thin blog posts from three years ago and serves them up ahead of the one asset you actually want buyers to find.

Now, of course, each one of these pieces made sense the day it shipped. Your content landfill grew from the best intentions, one reasonable decision at a time.

## Same as it ever was

And you may ask yourself, well, how did I get here?

That [Talking Heads song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Once_in_a_Lifetime_(Talking_Heads_song)) is the closest thing content marketing has to an official audit anthem. And the answer to David Byrne’s question is the most obvious word in business: incentives.

For a decade, the mandate handed to just about every content team was volume.

Feed the algorithm. Hit the SEO target. Fill the campaign calendar. Fuel the nurture tracks.

Your team was measured on output and rewarded for shipping content.

In those days, the content team of one of the biggest B2B brands around earned bonuses based on how many “articles” they published to one of the industry vertical blogs the company owned.

If the Media & Entertainment blog manager didn’t bite, the practitioners would search and replace the jargon words and try again with the manager of the Manufacturing blog.

Rinse. Repeat. Yawn.

At no point in that decade did the company reward anyone for auditing, pruning, or deleting.

Deletion wasn’t just unrewarded. It earned suspicion. Suggest taking something down, and people would worry that you’d destroy SEO “equity.”

Related:[5 Ideas To Reset Your 2026 Content Program](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/content-operations/reset-content-marketing-program)

The cry would echo through the cubes, “What about the long tail?!”

So the treadmill kept running, and everything it produced stayed exactly where it landed.

Leaders filed cleanup under housekeeping: an operational chore for a slow week that never came. Same as it ever was.

## Content flowing underground

But one thing did change over the last two years. AI handed the treadmill a supercharged motor.

The tools your team adopted to “scale content production” scaled it, all right.

You can now feed in a prompt that is literally as simple as “write a story about [any topic],” and generative AI will dutifully fulfill your wish. AI has made publishing functionally free, which means the landfill now grows at a speed no human maintenance effort can match.

And that’s why the increase in publishing speed is also an uptick in content-debt accumulation velocity.

Your capacity to create went up tenfold. Your capacity to keep it all true to the story went down.

## What is content debt, anyway?

In a nutshell, content debt is the compounding cost of neglecting to maintain, update, or delete outdated, unaligned, irrelevant, or inaccurate content.

The term owes a debt (heh) to software engineer Ward Cunningham, who introduced a metaphor still used in the tech industry.

Related:[For More Impact, Shift Content Operations to Content Orchestration in 2026](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/strategy-planning/content-orchestration-vs-operations)

Shipping quick-and-dirty code, he said, [is like taking on debt](http://wiki.c2.com/?WardExplainsDebtMetaphor). His point was that a little debt speeds you up — as long as you pay it back promptly.

Leave it unpaid and the interest compounds, until entire engineering teams spend their days servicing old shortcuts instead of building anything new. He called it [“technical debt.”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_debt)

The discipline that grew around the technical debt concept explains why serious software organizations now routinely run refactoring sprints and deprecation schedules.

(There’s another fascinating thread about how AI and vibe coding are now [accelerating technical debt](https://getautonoma.com/blog/vibe-coding-technical-debt), but that’s a different story.)

Marketing took out similar loans. And AI-driven content is costing us all an incredible amount of interest.

Think of it this way: Every asset you publish carries some level of maintenance obligation, a small recurring promise that it will stay true, current, and findable. Unpaid, that obligation compounds into contradiction, confusion, and risk.

That 2021 pricing PDF isn’t clutter. It’s an interest payment you make every day that inaccurate document stays up. And you pay in the currency of trust and deeper relationships with your audience.

Content debt is even more insidious than its software cousin because the impact doesn’t show up in your dashboard. It surfaces in other metrics, in departments that will never think to blame the resource center.

Just ask Air Canada. In 2024, its website chatbot told a grieving customer he could apply for a bereavement refund after his flight, but the airline's current[policy page](https://www.bbc.com/travel/article/20240222-air-canada-chatbot-misinformation-what-travellers-should-know) said the opposite.

As to the scale of the loan in marketing and sales, we’ve known for years that something like 60% to 70% of B2B marketing [content goes unused](https://www.forrester.com/report/from-assets-to-answers-implementing-the-forrester-b2b-buyer-question-to-content-framework/RES180602). And AI has not made that number better.

I’m certainly [not the first to talk about content debt](https://www.cmswire.com/digital-marketing/drowning-in-content-debt-heres-your-lifeline/). And I hope I’m not the last, because the explosiveness of AI in marketing operations means every marketing team needs to face it head-on.

## No longer letting the days go by

A marketing team from a challenger financial services tech brand came to me a while back with three years of accumulated content they suspected had turned into a debt that outweighed their assets.

Earlier this year, that client told me they’d finally opened the warehouse.

They examined three years of publishing aimed at four distinct audience personas: executive decision makers, technical influencers, customer success teams, and internal customers (including sales teams).

When they laid the inventory out, nobody on the team could say with confidence which assets still told the truth because no one had ever been asked to figure it out.

A content creator owned every asset, but only until the piece went live.

Once they examined everything, they found product names that no longer existed described in the present tense. They found claims that contradicted the current sales deck. Worse, they found old security documentation that no longer applied and ROI estimates from the early days of the pandemic recovery and cryptomania.

Nobody had been careless. The company had simply kept every promise it ever published without checking whether it still mattered (or even applied to) today.

## How to assess your content debt

If your organization has been publishing for a decade, you’re likely sitting on thousands of assets.

A [meaningful content audit](https://contentmarketinginstitute.com/content-operations/reset-content-marketing-program) is real work. Nobody is going to read 3,000 pieces of content and determine their current value by Friday.

But there are two ways to start to reduce your debt. Pick the one that matches the depth of your landfill.

### Level 1: The machine audit

Here’s some irony: The same AI technology that supercharged the content treadmill is the perfect candidate to send to read your entire library.

A human team auditing 3,000 assets is a quarter-long project that dies in week three. A well-instructed model can read all of it in a few hours and hand back the things you actually need:

- Every asset’s last-verified date

- Every claim with a price, statistic, or product name attached

- Clusters of near duplicates

- Contradiction pairs (every place where two of your assets disagree with each other or with your current messaging document)

When you join that output with a year of analytics, you have the full debt schedule.

Remember: AI flags, but people verify.

The model cannot know what’s true at your company this quarter or where you may intentionally set different expectations or play outside of brand rules.

Don’t let the machine that dug the landfill certify it clean.

### Level 2: The default-dead rule

For those of you on teams whose debt is so large that the audit itself is unaffordable, there’s a blunt instrument (and it’s one I’ve self-prescribed): Flip the burden of proof.

Declare that anything older than the last 24 months is presumed wrong until someone proves otherwise.

Some teams operationalize this with an archive-first approach. Everything older than a year or two gets archived by default, and an asset earns its way back only when a named owner reverifies it.

That sounds reckless until you remember that decade-old claim: If 60% of the warehouse was never used, most of it will never be missed.

Remember to keep the guardrails. Allow exemptions for compliance and legal pages and send genuinely evergreen assets to get reverified rather than archived. And make sure everything archived gets redirected so that links don’t return 404 errors.

Whichever level you choose, resist the urge to fix anything yet. This week is diagnosis; the treatment plan comes next. Doing things in the wrong order is how cleanup projects die.

## Am I right or am I wrong?

I can hear your objection from here: “My leadership will never let me stop creating to clean up.”

You’re right. They won’t agree if you make the request using the language of hygiene.

“We should spend time tidying up the CMS.”

“Our tags are a mess.”

“The resource center needs a refresh.”

Each of these claims will get ignored — and they should because they sound like housekeeping issues competing with a growth mandate.

But what if you phrased it in financial terms?

What if you described the landfill as expensive rather than messy? What if you talked about the debt’s impact on pipeline and deals that die late and unexplained?

That’s not a hypothetical.

It’s your story. Maintain it well.
