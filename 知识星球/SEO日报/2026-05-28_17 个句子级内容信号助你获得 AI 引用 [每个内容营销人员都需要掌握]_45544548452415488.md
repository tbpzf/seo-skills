Let's start with the first section, structuring for retrieval.

Structuring for Retrieval

The first five signals happen at the document level, before you write a single sentence of body copy. They determine where your key ideas sit, how sections are organized, and what the model finds when it first scans the page.

1. Front-load the answer in the first 30% of the piece

Kevin Indig's February 2026 analysis of 1.2 million ChatGPT citations found a pattern he calls the ski ramp. The first 30% of your content generates 44.2% of all citations. The middle 40% generates 31.1%. The final 30% — where most writers build toward their conclusion — generates only 24.7%.

LLMs are trained heavily on journalism and academic writing, both of which open with the conclusion. The model has learned to treat the intro as the most information-dense part of any document. It grabs the who, what, and where from the top, then uses that frame to interpret everything below.

If your key insight is in paragraph eight, the model is 2.5 times less likely to cite it than if it were in paragraph two. Write the answer first. Move it to the top of the draft even if you discovered it last. Treat the first 500 words as load-bearing. Everything after elaborates.

✓ Action: If a reader stops after your opening paragraph, they have the answer.

2. Turn every H2 into a literal question readers would actually type

Semrush's January 2026 study of 337,785 URLs found that Q&A formatting showed a +25.45% correlation with AI citations, the third-highest positive factor in the study. Indig's data adds a layer of specificity: 78.4% of citation-bearing questions came from H2 or H3 headers, not from paragraph text.

The reason is structural. The model treats the H2 as the user's prompt and the paragraph immediately below as the generated response. When your header reads like a real question someone would type into ChatGPT, the model's retrieval pattern matches your page's structure. When it reads like an abstract topic label, the model has to work harder to extract a usable answer.

❌ Loses: The History of SEO ✅ Wins: When did SEO start?

✓ Action: Every H2 in your piece is a question a real person would actually type.

3. Echo the entity in the first sentence of every answer

This is a direct extension of item two, and it deserves its own line because the habit is easy to build. Indig calls it "entity echoing": the subject of your H2 question should appear as the subject of the first sentence of the answer.

If your H2 is "What is programmatic SEO?", the first word of the paragraph below should be "Programmatic SEO." Not "It." Not "This technique." The model reads your H2 as the user prompt. It then looks for the strongest semantic path to an answer. A sentence that starts with the subject of the question is that path. A sentence that starts with "It" requires the model to resolve the reference first, adding friction.

❌ Loses: When did SEO start? / "It began in the early 1990s..." 

✅ Wins: When did SEO start? / "SEO started in the early 1990s..."

✓ Action: The subject of every H2 question appears as the subject of the first sentence below it.

4. Add a structured TL;DR directly after the H1

Semrush's January 2026 study flagged "clarity and summarization" as the single strongest positive correlate of AI citations, at +32.83% across 337,785 URLs. The top optimization recommendation Semrush issued on the back of that finding: add a brief, structured summary at the beginning of the page that clearly states the key takeaway.

This is structurally different from item one. Item one is about your prose introduction, the opening paragraph that leads with the answer. This is a separate structural element: a bulleted list of three to five key takeaways placed between your H1 title and your first H2. Not a decorative callout box with generic copy. The takeaways themselves.

The TL;DR gives AI systems a compressed, extractable version of the full piece. It also tells the human reader, before they have read a word of body copy, whether this piece is worth their time. Both readers benefit from the same thing.

✓ Action: Your TL;DR would stand alone as a LinkedIn post if you cut everything below it.

5. One idea per section, one section per idea

Semrush's study found that "section structure" correlated with AI citations at +22.91%. The finding is intuitive once you understand how LLMs extract information. A cited section is a self-contained unit. A section that mixes two or three ideas is extraction debt; the model has to synthesize across the section to produce an answer, and synthesis increases the probability of hallucination or omission.

The move is to split mixed sections. If your H2 covers both "why you need structured data" and "how to implement it," those are two sections. Give each a header, a claim, and a direct answer.

✓ Action: Every section has one H2 and one core claim. If you find two claims under one header, that is two sections.

Sentence-Level Signals

The next five signals happen at the sentence level. They determine how the model reads individual paragraphs and which sentences it extracts as citable units.

6. Open definitions with "is," "are," or "means"

Indig's February 2026 analysis found that cited paragraphs contain definitional language — "is," "refers to," "is defined as" — nearly twice as often as skipped paragraphs. Cited text showed this pattern 36.2% of the time; skipped text showed it 20.2%.

The mechanic is rooted in how LLMs handle vector retrieval. In a vector database, "is" acts as a strong bridge between a subject and its definition. When a user asks "What is X?", the model searches for the strongest semantic path to an answer. A direct "X is Y" sentence is that path. A hedged "X may be considered as a form of Y that has emerged in response to changing conditions..." is not.

❌ Loses: "AEO may be considered as an emerging discipline that has developed in response to the shifting nature of search." 

✅ Wins: "AEO is the practice of optimizing content for AI citations."

✓ Action: Every definition in your piece starts with "is," "are," or "means."

7. Write at college level — not grade 8, and not PhD level

The most common myth in AEO/GEO writing advice is to aim for a grade 8 reading level or lower. The data says otherwise.

Indig's February 2026 citation analysis found that winning pages, the ones LLMs actually cite, sit at a Flesch-Kincaid grade of approximately 16. Losing pages sit at 19.1. Grade 8 is consumer magazine territory. Grade 19 is unreadable academic prose. The target is grade 16. Think HBR, The Economist, or Stratechery: confident writing for intelligent adults.

The reason PhD-level writing loses is mechanical. Long sentences filled with multisyllable jargon are harder for models to extract clean facts from. The reason grade-8 writing loses for business content is different; it signals a lack of expertise. The winning register is business-grade prose: clear, declarative, professional, with no unnecessary complexity.

Run your draft through the Hemingway App as a reference point, then ignore its score if it pushes you below grade 10. Aim for confidence, not simplicity.

✓ Action: If Hemingway App scores your draft below grade 10, you have overcorrected.

8. Strike an analyst tone — not Wikipedia, not hot take

Indig's 2026 analysis measured the Subjectivity score of cited text on the NLP scale from 0.0 to 1.0, where 0.0 is pure fact and 1.0 is pure opinion. The median Subjectivity score of cited content: 0.47. The precise middle of the scale.

Pure-fact writing scores around 0.1. It reads like Wikipedia. It states data without telling you what the data means. Models can extract it, but it does not serve users who need interpretation, not just information. Pure-opinion writing scores around 0.9. It makes strong claims with no factual anchor. Models treat it as too risky to cite; it cannot be verified against anything.

The winning register is analyst voice: a fact paired immediately with what that fact means.

❌ Wikipedia voice (loses): "The iPhone 15 uses the A16 chip." 

❌ Hot-take voice (loses): "The iPhone 15 is an absolutely stunning piece of engineering." 

✅ Analyst voice (wins): "The iPhone 15 uses the A16 chip, which makes it the stronger pick for content creators shooting in low light."

✓ Action: Every significant fact in your piece is followed by a sentence telling the reader what to do with it.

9. Pack your content with named entities

Indig's analysis found that cited text has an entity density of 20.6%. Normal English prose sits at 5-8%, a benchmark derived from the Brown Corpus and Penn Treebank. Cited content is roughly three times more entity-dense than baseline writing.

Named entities, that is, specific tools, brands, people, studies, dates, product names — serve as anchors. They reduce what NLP researchers call "perplexity" in the model's response. A sentence with three proper nouns is grounded and verifiable. A sentence with zero is a risk the model will not take.

This applies to competitors too. Don't sanitize the category. A comparison that mentions Salesforce, HubSpot, and Pipedrive earns more citations than one that mentions "leading CRM platforms."

❌ Loses: "Several leading platforms offer AI citation tracking." 

✅ Wins: "Profound, Ahrefs Brand Radar, and Peec AI all offer AI citation tracking."

✓ Action: Every 300-word section contains at least three proper nouns.

10. Strip hedge words from every claim

Hedge words exist to protect the writer from being wrong. They also make the sentence unusable as a citation. 

Semrush's January 2026 study found "clarity and summarization" at +32.83% correlation, the highest positive factor in their analysis. Part of what Semrush measured as clarity is the absence of qualifiers that make claims slippery.

A model citing your content needs a claim it can stand behind. A sentence that hedges its own claim is a sentence the model will skip. "Arguably," "perhaps," "it could be said that," "many experts believe," "it is often thought that" — each one dilutes the sentence it appears in.

❌ Loses: "It could be argued that many content marketers have perhaps underutilized structured data." 

✅ Wins: "Most content marketers underutilize structured data."

✓ Action: Ctrl-F your draft for "arguably," "perhaps," "many believe," and "it is often thought." Rewrite the sentence to sound more confident.

Credibility Signals

LLMs are not just extracting answers. They are assessing whether a source is trustworthy enough to stake their response on. These three signals determine whether your content passes that assessment.

11. Cite primary sources with dates and sample sizes

Semrush's January 2026 study found that E-E-A-T signals showed a +30.64% correlation with AI citations, the second-highest factor in their 337,785-URL analysis. One of the strongest E-E-A-T signals is attribution quality.

"Recent research" is weasel language. The model cannot verify it. "Semrush's January 2026 analysis of 337,785 URLs" is a citation. It has a name, a date, and a sample size. The model can check those details and weight the claim accordingly. This is also why the distinction between primary and secondary sources matters for AEO/GEO content. Citing a blog post that cites a study is weaker than citing the study itself. Go upstream.

✓ Action: Every stat in your piece has a named study, a sample size, and a year.

12. Give your author bio a specific credential, not a job title

Semrush's E-E-A-T findings include author signals as a measured component of the citation correlation. A job title tells the model what role you hold. A specific credential tells the model what you know.

"Content strategist with five years of experience" is a job description. "Content strategist who has built AI-aware content programs for B2B SaaS companies including X and Y" is a credential. The model can locate the second in its training data. It cannot do anything useful with the first.

✓ Action: Your author bio includes at least one specific, verifiable credential: a company, a publication, or a measurable outcome.

13. Include one original data point, firsthand example, or named case study per section

The third component of Semrush's E-E-A-T finding is original content signals. A sentence that only you or your client could write is, by definition, a sentence no other page contains. That exclusivity is both a credibility signal and a differentiation signal.

The practical standard is narrow. "When we reduced client page load from 4.2 seconds to 1.8 seconds, organic traffic increased 43% within two months" is a citable unit with a specific number, a timeframe, and a measurable outcome. "Our approach works well" is not.

✓ Action: Every section contains at least one example no other writer could replicate.

Format and Intent

Before you pick a format, classify the query. Most format decisions that hurt AEO/GEO performance happen when writers reach for the format they are most comfortable with rather than the format the query calls for.

14. Match your content format to the intent of the query

Wix Studio's AI Search Lab published a March 2026 analysis of 75,000 AI answers and more than one million citations across ChatGPT, Google AI Mode, and Perplexity. The headline finding: query intent, not industry and not AI platform, is the strongest predictor of which content format gets cited.

Informational queries cite articles 45.5% of the time and listicles 21.7%. Commercial queries cite listicles 40.9% of the time. Transactional and navigational queries favor product and category pages, roughly 40% combined.

Answering "what is AEO?" with a listicle of ten AEO tools is a format mismatch. The model is looking for an explanation, not a ranking. Answering "best AEO tools" with a 3,000-word definitional piece is equally wrong. The model is looking for a structured comparison, not an essay.

✓ Action: Before writing, classify the query as informational, commercial, transactional, or navigational. Write the format that category calls for.

15. In "best of" lists, write as a third party — even if you are in the category

The same Wix Studio study found that in professional services, third-party listicles account for 80.9% of listicle citations. Self-promotional listicles account for 19.1%. That four-to-one gap represents the model's calibration against bias.

If you write a "best AEO tools" list and your own service appears at the top, the model reads a promotional document. If your service appears honestly alongside named alternatives with specific trade-offs stated plainly, the model reads editorial content. 

Seer Interactive's February 2026 analysis of more than 2 million ChatGPT citations adds a practical floor: listicle citations dropped 30% between December 2025 and January 2026 as platforms began penalizing self-promotional lists. The format still works. Self-promotional execution is what is losing ground.

✓ Action: If your product or service appears in a list you authored, it is not ranked first, and the piece reads as if a third party could have written it.

16. Write a headline that names the outcome, not just the topic

Kevin Indig's April 2026 study with AirOps, analyzing 16,851 ChatGPT queries and 353,799 pages across ten industries, found that pages with headlines that directly answer the query get cited 41% of the time. Pages with loosely related headlines drop to 29%. That twelve-point gap comes entirely from how well the title signals the answer before the model or the reader has read a word of body copy.

"The Ultimate Guide to LinkedIn Growth" is a container. It names the topic but not the payoff. "5 Posting Tactics to Grow Your LinkedIn Follower Count" is a direct answer. It names what you leave with. The first headline tells you only what is inside. The second tells you whether it is worth going in.

✓ Action: Read your headline aloud. It should name the outcome, not just the topic.

Maintenance
17. Update existing content before you write something new

Ahrefs' November 2025 analysis of the top 1,000 most-cited ChatGPT pages found that 82% of non-Wikipedia cited pages had been updated in 2025. The data was collected in September 2025, meaning most of those updates happened within the same calendar year as the citation. ChatGPT is demonstrably biased toward recent content. Freshness is not a vanity metric.

The production implication: before you commission a new piece on a topic you have already covered, ask whether updating the existing piece would be more effective. An updated article with a refreshed publication date, new statistics, and a revised TL;DR often outperforms a new article starting from zero authority signals and zero inbound links.

✓ Action: Your content calendar includes an update cadence, not only a new-publication schedule.

What all 17 of These Signals Come Down to 

The content signals that improve AI citations aren’t exactly some novel finding to any content marketer who's been around for a minute. 

They are practices good writing coaches have told good writers since we began writing on the internet. 

What’s new is the added cost of ignoring them: invisibility in AI search.
